[I]t contains an admirable comic force, which springs from the contrast between the texture of the discourse, which is the rugged, sober, and laconic Piedmontese dialect, never written except on a bet, and the Hebrew inlay, snatched from the language of the fathers, sacred and solemn, geologic, polished smooth by the millenia like the bed of a glacier. But this conflict reflects another, the essential conflict of the Judaism of the Diaspora, scattered among the Gentiles, that is, the goyim, torn between their divine vocation and the daily misery of existence; and still another, even more general, which is inherent in the human condition, since man is a centaur, a tangle of flesh and mind, divine inspiration and dust.                    — Primo Levi, The Periodic Table, speaking of a Hebraic Piedmontese dialect spoken by a small band of Italian Jews in the story “Argon”
