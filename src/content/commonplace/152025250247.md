> Francis Ponge, “Banks of the Loire” Roanne, 24th May 1941May nothing from now on make me go back on my word: never to sacrifice the object of my effort to the profit of some verbal windfall I have set up in passing, nor to arranging such windfall into poems.To go back always only to the object itself, to what is crude, different: different in particular from what I have just (at any given moment) written of it. May my work be a continual righting of my expression (without any prior regard to the form of that expression) for the sake of the crude object.So, writing on the Loire of a place on the banks of this river, I should without let keep diving eyes and mind back into it. Whenever I’ve got hung up on an expression, dive back into the flow of the river. To recognize the supreme right of the object, its inalienable right, as against any poem … No poem being ever without a minima appeal on the part of the object of the poem, nor without the charge of being called counterfeit.The object is always more important, more interesting, more capable (endowed with rights): it has no duty in regard to me, it is I who have all obligation to it. What the preceding lines dont enough emphasize: ergo, never to stop at poetic form - this advance, however, to be utilized at a certain moment in my effort because it offers a play of mirrors which can make certain otherwise obscure facets of the object come dear. The accidence of words, verbal analogies, are one of the ways of perceiving the object.Never try to arrange things. Things and poems are irreconcilable. It’s a matter of knowing whether one wants to make a poem or report a thing (in the hope that the mind may attain it, make in relation to it some new step).It’s the second alternative that my taste ( a violent taste for things, and for intellectual advances) unhesitatingly makes me choose.I have made up my mind …And it dont much matter to me now if someone wants to call what comes of it all poetry. For myself, the least suspicion of poetic purring warns me simply that I’m back once more on the merrygoround and have to get off.–translation by Cid Corman, published in Origin 2:11, October 1963.
