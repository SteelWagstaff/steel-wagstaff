Apart from mats woven patternless, with apparently single-minded concentration on practical utility, they had nothing—no tools carved with patterns or images, no daubing, no dyed garments, not even music or dance. When he asked them why, they replied that they had abandoned them in their ‘time of sadness’. This surely does not mean that these people were incapable of art; rather, they chose not to make symbols or images or evocations of things not present. Can the apparent absence of what we recognize as art among the non-human animals be of the same sort? … there should in principle be no reason why we should not look at the behavior of other species and ask [‘Is it art?’] unprejudically.                    — Felipe Fernández-Armesto in Humankind: A Brief History, describing Walter Grainge White’s studies of the Mawken (oran laut) ‘sea people’ of the Bay of Bengal. I have, however, found a problem with Armesto’s reporting of this fact. His source, Grainge White, actually wrote about the Mawken’s dance and musical performances, which he witnessed. They did, however, tell him: “These are the days of sadness. Mawken people seldom dance and play now. We do not make the musical instruments as in the long ago.”
