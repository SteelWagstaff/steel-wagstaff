> The contemporary poetry most honored by establishment journals and national prizes tends, with some intimidating exceptions, to manifest one of two basic styles. The first, as we have seen, is dominated by residues of the sublime, either in the personal intensities characteristic of the confessional tradition or in various explorations of the shift inward from perception to mythic levels of awareness, in the mode of deep image poetry or in Merwin&rsquo;s privative sublime, where absence from self is the vehicle for mystery. The second mode is self-consciously anti-mythic, stressing attention to craft and tonal control of dramatic materials. But in rejecting the mythic, this mode rejects the various forms for exploring immediacy basic to modernist writing and is willing to return to a traditional sense of poetry as well-made, metaphoric meditation. Where the first mode tends to collapse the sublime into the ridiculous, the second runs the risk of displacing all traces of sincerity and the will to truth in its concern for artifice and for moral discriminations that often express a narcissim of moral sensitivity where composition collapses into merely formal composure.

— Charles Altieri, <a href="https:::://www.jstor.org/stable/pdfplus/25303858.pdf">&ldquo;The Objectivist Tradition,&rdquo;</a> _Chicago Review _30.3, Winter 1979 (page 19)
